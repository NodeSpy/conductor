Ten principles for reading at a glance

conductor produces runs, cases, approvals, logs, diffs and bot chatter around the clock. A person needs to answer three questions in seconds: does anything need me, is anything broken, and is everything else fine? Every direction below follows these ten rules. They change the content model, not the paint. The “before” examples are verbatim from the current prototype; the “after” examples are set in the recommended direction.

§

Three directions

Each direction is a value set for the same semantic tokens as tokens.json. The mocks are written once against those token names, so switching direction is a value swap, not a rebuild. All three palettes pass the round-one contrast rules (456 pairs, 0 failures, from v2/check.mjs), and the categorical chart palette validates against each direction’s surfaces (v2/dataviz-validation.txt).

Chosen: Daylight

Daylight was chosen. It is now the design system: see the Daylight brand book and BRAND.md. The logo is still open on the logo review. The recommendation below is kept as written.

Ship Daylight. It answers all three review notes directly: it reads as a product rather than a terminal, it has a recognisable brand voice, and it is built for legibility from the typeface up.

Why it works for the newcomer

Sam, the newcomer audit’s persona, couldn’t find a first step and couldn’t say what bots may do without asking. Daylight leads every screen with a sentence (“5 things need you. Everything else is running normally.”) and puts plain-language titles above the technical ones. Its typeface, Atkinson Hyperlegible, was designed by the Braille Institute so that easily confused characters (1/l/I, 0/O, rn/m) stay distinct, which matters for ids and numbers read at a glance. The warm surfaces make empty states and onboarding feel welcoming rather than daunting.

Why it works for the power user

Riley, the power-user audit’s persona, needs density and precision. Under Daylight’s friendliness is the same discipline as Broadsheet: tabular figures, right-aligned durations and costs, ids kept in mono at the end of every row, colour only on rows that need attention, and a compact density (36px rows) as a token set. The history table carries exactly the same columns as Broadsheet’s; only the shell is softer.

What to borrow from the others

The principles are shared, so the best information patterns travel. Later decisions: the overview opens with At a glance (a few key counts), and the Activity page shows runs over time as one aggregate plus the exceptions by workflow, which scales where one lane per workflow didn’t. Neither changes Daylight’s visual language.

Runner-up

Broadsheet is the strongest pure information design, and the one Riley would choose. But it reads as an editorial product for experts, and its starkness asks more of a newcomer. Score is the most distinctive brand. But making the timeline the main view everywhere adds a metaphor to learn, and that suits a single Activity view better than every screen.
